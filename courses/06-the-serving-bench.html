Part 1 · covers API, managed endpoint and self-hosting

Three ways to pay for a token

4 min live

An API bills per token, so cost rises in a straight line with traffic and an idle hour costs nothing. A managed endpoint, such as Hugging Face Inference Endpoints, bills per GPU-hour whether the GPU is busy or not. Running the engine yourself on a rented GPU bills the same way, plus the people who keep it up. The fine-tuning course drew the sloped line against the flat one; this bench asks what the flat line actually serves.

LiveThe unit that makes them comparable2 min▶

Price per million tokens and price per GPU-hour cannot be compared until you know how many requests the GPU serves per hour at the latency your users will accept. That is what sessions 3 and 4 built: the capacity widget finds the highest load at which the slowest five percent of first tokens still arrive within 1 second. Divide the hourly price by the requests served in that hour and you have cost per 1,000 requests, the same unit as the API rows.

The catch is in the figure's last line. The GPU's $0.15 holds only when the GPU runs at 1.5 requests a second all month. Kiln's easy traffic peaks at 1.02 requests a second (the plan widget prints it), so most of the month the L4 is part-idle and its real cost is the flat $584. The plan widget below prices that honestly: it counts whole GPUs, sized for the peak.

Self-studyWhich engine, if you run it yourself3 min▶

Read on 2026-11-08: vLLM (Apache-2.0) describes itself as "a high-throughput and memory-efficient inference and serving engine for LLMs", with PagedAttention, automatic prefix caching and chunked prefill on by default in its V1 engine. SGLang lists "RadixAttention, prefix caching, and multi-GPU parallelism". NVIDIA's TensorRT-LLM lists in-flight batching and a "Paged KV cache with intelligent block reuse". Hugging Face's own docs now say "text-generation-inference is now in maintenance mode" and point to vLLM and SGLang. A managed endpoint runs one of these for you; self-hosting means you pick, upgrade and page someone at night. Re-verify every engine's changelog before you commit.

Real world

Two prices for the same GPU on one vendor's site. Hugging Face's pricing page, read on 2026-11-08, lists an H100 80GB endpoint on AWS at $4.50 an hour; its Inference Endpoints documentation lists H100 on GCP only, at $10 an hour. The bench uses $4.50 and says where it came from. A serving plan that hangs on one hourly price should quote the page, the date and the region, because the cheaper reading may not be the one you can buy.

The bench

Three levers, each computed on its own

8 min live

Each widget is one part of the bench, with every number computed in your browser by the same engine an independent Python script reproduces: 2,452 values, all equal. Run each one at Kiln's settings before stacking them.

Part A · the router

Learned router, threshold 0.87, gpt-4.1 prices: the widget should print 59.0% sent small, 95.7% acceptable, $2.49 per 1,000 requests and a 45.9% saving. Then tick the break button and watch the same threshold fall to 92.7% acceptable.

Part B · capacity

L4, Llama 3.1 8B, FP8 weights, BF16 KV cache, continuous batching, paged KV, prefix cache on. Run the sweep: the highest load meeting the 1 s target is 1.5 requests a second, $0.15 per 1,000 requests at that load. Turn the prefix cache off and the same GPU manages 0.75.

Part C · the cache

Pick gpt-4.1, static first, 90% hits: $2.98 per 1,000 against $4.60 with no caching, 35.2% saved. Learn AI Observability does the accounting of cache tokens on an invoice (what AI costs you ↗); this part is the design decision behind it.

Part 2 · covers stacking the levers

Levers do not add up, they multiply down

4 min live

Each lever works on what the previous one left. The cache only discounts the requests that still go to the big model; the router decides how many those are; the GPU only has to carry what the router sends it. So the order you add them changes how much each one appears to save, and the total is not the sum of their solo savings.

LiveRead the plan widget's five presets in order2 min▶

Cache only saves 35.2 percent: every request stays on gpt-4.1 and its 1,200-token system prompt is billed at the cached price on 90 percent of requests. The router with gpt-4.1-mini on the easy side saves 45.9 percent. Moving the easy side from gpt-4.1-mini ($882 a month) to one L4 ($584) takes the saving to 50.2 percent, not because the GPU is cheaper per token at Kiln's volume but because one GPU covers the easy side's peak. Adding the cache to the big side gives the full stack: $2,436, 64.7 percent under today, at 95.7 percent acceptable answers.

Note what the cache saves inside the full stack: $2,850 of big-side spend becomes $1,852, about $1,000 a month. Alone it saved $2,430. The router took most of the traffic it would have discounted.

Self-studyWhere the bigger GPU loses2 min▶

Choose "Full stack on an H100" in the plan widget. The H100 serves 12 requests a second at the target, eight times the L4, and at full load its $0.10 per 1,000 beats the L4's $0.15. Kiln's easy side does not fill it, so the bill is the flat $3,285 a month and the plan lands at $5,137, only 25.5 percent under today. Capacity you cannot fill is the most expensive capacity there is. The L4's limit is the right one to hit first; the H100 is the plan for when the easy side outgrows several L4s.

Real world

Published routing savings are each their own bench. RouteLLM (Ong et al. 2024) reports routers that "reduce costs by over 2 times without sacrificing response quality", with a cost-saving ratio of 3.66x on MT Bench at half the quality gap recovered but 1.41x on MMLU. FrugalGPT (Chen et al. 2023) reports matching GPT-4 "with up to 98% cost reduction" on its tasks. Kiln's cheap router saves 45.9 percent at a 95 percent floor on 2,000 RouteLLM rows. None of these is wrong; each is a different task mix, quality bar and pair of models. Your own floor on your own rows is the only number that belongs in your memo.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

Two plans that look cheapest and fail

3 min live

A cost chart rewards whatever spends least, so the two traps sit at the bottom of it. One sends everything to the small model. The other keeps the router but with its scores scrambled, which still splits the traffic in the same proportion and so still looks like routing. Both are cheaper than the full stack. Both fail the floor.

LiveWhy the break button matters2 min▶

The scrambled router sends exactly the same share of traffic to the small model as the real one, 59.0 percent at threshold 0.87, because it uses the same scores, just attached to the wrong rows. So the bill barely moves and the quality falls from 95.7 to 92.7 percent. That proves the router carries a real signal: without it, the same split costs quality. A bench that cannot show this cannot tell a working router from a coin. It also says how weak the cheap router is: its area under the curve on the 2,000 rows is 0.662, where 0.5 is a coin. The cheapest threshold that clears the floor with a scrambled router is 0.91, saving only 26.7 percent.

Self-studyThe ceiling nobody can buy2 min▶

In the router widget, "Hindsight" routes on the judge's score itself: small exactly when the small model's answer scored 4 or 5. It sends 87.1 percent small, ships no weak answers and costs $1.44 per 1,000, a 68.7 percent saving. No router sees the judge's score before answering, so this is the ceiling, not a plan. The gap between the cheap router's 45.9 percent and the ceiling's 68.7 percent is what a better router could earn, and it is the honest budget for building one.

Capstone

Kiln's decision memo

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Constructed Kiln example. Maya's memo is one page with six lines, written for Theo and whoever signs the bill, and every number in it is one the bench printed. Run the plan widget through its presets first, then write the memo for your own product as you go.

Write the decision as one line someone could disagree with. Staged, with a date, and with the cheapest safe step first.

★ Line 1 · decisionStage 1 now: re-lay out the prompt static-first (cache only). Stage 2 next month: router at 0.87, easy side on one own L4, FP8 weights, prefix cache. Re-run the bench before stage 2.

Report quality against the floor, with the router's own error. Copy what the router widget printed.

★ Line 2 · qualityOn 2,000 held-out real prompts: 95.7% acceptable at threshold 0.87 (floor 95%). 59.0% go small; 87 weak answers per 2,000 reach users. Scrambled router at the same split: 92.7%.

Give the bill at Kiln's volume, with the prices dated and the model labelled.

★ Line 3 · costAt 1,500,000 requests a month: today $6,896. Stage 1 $4,466 (35.2% saved). Stage 2 $2,436 (64.7% saved): $1,852 API, $584 for one L4. List prices read 2026-11-08. GPU capacity is a model (1.5 req/s at a 1 s p95 first-token target).

Name what would flip the plan, in both directions. This is the line that makes the memo honest.

★ Line 4 · flip conditionsHarder NO to self-hosting if: a load test on the real L4 serves under 1.02 req/s, the easy side's peak, at the target (gpt-4.1-mini, $3,732 a month, is the stage-2 plan). Do not drop the router to save money: everything small fails the floor at 87.1%.

State what self-hosting adds that the bench does not price, and the review.

★ Lines 5 and 6 · risk and reviewNot in the numbers: on-call, upgrades, a second L4 for failover. If those cost more than $298 a month, gpt-4.1-mini wins stage 2. Review: after 30 days of stage 1, owner Maya, with Theo.

The arithmetic in line 5. Router plus gpt-4.1-mini is $3,732 a month; router plus one own L4 is $3,434. The difference, $298, is the most the people and spare hardware for self-hosting can cost before the API path is cheaper. Write that number down for your own plan: it is usually the deciding one, and no price list shows it.

Homework

After tonight

45 min
  1. Finish your six lines for your own product, with your own floor and your own volume. Every number must be one you can point to in a run.
  2. Label 200 of your own requests as "a small model's answer would be fine" or not, and measure any router you are considering against that set before you trust a published saving.
  3. Load-test one GPU with your real prompt and answer lengths before you believe any capacity number, including this bench's. Compare its p95 time to first token with the model's.
  4. Find your self-hosting break-even: the monthly people and failover cost at which your API path wins, as in line 5.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The everything-small plan costs $1,168 a month against the full stack's $2,436. Why does the memo reject it?

The floor comes before the price. On the same 2,000 real rows, the small model's answer scored 4 or 5 for 87.1% of prompts, so sending everything small ships 259 weak answers per 2,000.

2 · Scrambling the router's scores keeps the bill almost the same. What does the drop from 95.7% to 92.7% acceptable prove?

The scrambled router sends the same 59.0% small, just the wrong 59.0%. The quality gap at the same split is the router's signal; a bench that could not show it could not tell a router from a coin.

3 · An H100 serves Kiln's easy side at $0.10 per 1,000 requests at full load, the L4 at $0.15. Why does the full stack on an H100 cost more?

Per-request cost at full load only matters if you can fill the GPU. At Kiln's volume one L4 carries the easy side's peak, so the full stack costs $2,436 on the L4 and $5,137 on the H100.
