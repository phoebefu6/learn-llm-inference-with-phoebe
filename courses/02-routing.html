Routing: not every question needs the big model

The cheapest decode step is the one a big model never takes. Plenty of requests ("rewrite this sentence", "what is our refund window") get a perfectly good answer from a model that costs a fraction as much. The hard part is knowing which ones before you have the answer. Tonight we look at the three ways teams pick a model per request, treat a router as what it really is, a classifier whose mistakes have a price, and route 2,000 real prompts at three thresholds to find the cheapest setting that still clears Kiln's quality floor.

Part 1 · covers fixed tiers and cascades

Three ways to pick a model per request

5 min live

There are only three places the decision can happen. Before any traffic, by a table: this feature or this customer plan gets this model. After a small model has answered, by a check: is this good enough, and if not, ask the big one. Or from the prompt alone, by a scorer that runs before any model does. Each shape pays for its decision in a different currency: the table pays in blindness, the cascade in latency, the router in its own mistakes.

LiveFixed tiers: a table, not a model3 min▶

The simplest router is a lookup. The free plan gets the small model; enterprise gets the big one. The autocomplete box gets the small model; the "analyse this spreadsheet" button gets the big one. Nothing is scored, nothing is trained, and the bill is easy to forecast because the mix of features is the mix of models.

Its weakness is that it is blind to the request. A paid customer asking for a one-line rewrite still pays the big model's price, and a free customer with a genuinely hard question gets a weak answer. Tiers are the right first move when your features differ sharply in difficulty. When difficulty varies inside one feature, as it does in Kiln's single chat box, a table cannot see it.

LiveCascades: ask small first, escalate on doubt3 min▶

A cascade sends every request to the cheap model first, then runs a check on the answer: a scorer, a confidence threshold, a rule. If the check passes, ship the cheap answer. If it fails, call the next, bigger model. The decision is made with more information than a router has, because it can look at an actual answer.

The price is paid twice over. Every request pays for the small call, including the ones that end up escalated. And an escalated request waits for the small call, the check and the big call one after another, so the slowest users get slower. For a chat box where time to first token is the complaint, that tail matters. For overnight batch work, it often does not.

Real world

FrugalGPT's cascade. Chen, Zaharia and Zou (2023) start from the observation that API fees "can differ by two orders of magnitude" between models, then chain models from cheap to expensive with a learned scorer deciding when to stop. On their tasks the cascade can "match the performance of the best individual LLM (e.g. GPT-4) with up to 98% cost reduction or improve the accuracy over GPT-4 by 4% with the same cost". Note the "up to" and "on their tasks": the saving is a property of the task mix as much as of the method, which is the thread through the rest of tonight.

Self-studyWhat a cascade really costs, in one line3 min▶

Write the cascade's cost per request as: small call, always paid, plus the check, plus the big call times the share of requests that escalate. Write its latency for an escalated request as: small call plus check plus big call. A router's cost is: scorer, plus whichever single model it picked. So a cascade beats a router on cost only when its check is much better at spotting failures than a router's guess, enough to outweigh paying the small model on every request. If your small model's answers are long, that always-paid small call is not small at all.

Part 2 · covers learned routers

A router is a classifier with a price tag

5 min live

A learned router reads the prompt, outputs a score between 0 and 1 for "the small model will do fine here", and compares it to a threshold. Above the threshold, small model; below it, big model. That is a binary classifier, and like every classifier it makes two kinds of mistake. Here each mistake has a price: one is paid by your users in quality, the other by you in dollars. The threshold is the dial that trades them.

LiveWhat RouteLLM actually trained4 min▶

Ong et al. (2024), "RouteLLM: Learning to Route LLMs with Preference Data", train routers between a strong model (gpt-4-1106-preview) and a weak one (Mixtral 8x7B). The training signal is human preference: 80k Chatbot Arena battles, about 65k comparisons after filtering, plus an augmentation of about 120K samples labelled by GPT-4 as judge, which cost them about $700. That price is worth remembering. Labelled routing data is cheap next to a month of big-model traffic.

They compare four routers: similarity-weighted ranking, matrix factorization, a BERT classifier and a causal LLM classifier. Their headline is that routing can "reduce costs by over 2 times without sacrificing response quality". They measure quality as PGR, the share of the gap between the weak and the strong model's score that the routed system recovers, and report cost savings at a given PGR. The code and the dataset are Apache-2.0, which is why the rows under tonight's widget are theirs.

LiveHow much routing saves depends on who measured3 min▶

Put the claims side by side and they do not agree, and they are not supposed to. The RouteLLM paper says "over 2 times". Its own Table 6, at 50% PGR, gives a cost-saving ratio of 3.66x on MT Bench but 1.41x on MMLU. The project's README says "up to 85%" (reported: a README claim, not a paper result). FrugalGPT says "up to 98%" on its tasks. Kiln's cheap router, on 2,000 RouteLLM rows with a 95% floor, saves 45.9% (computed, below).

Each number is its own benchmark with its own quality bar and its own price gap. None of them is your number. The professional move is to hold the range in your head and measure on your own traffic, which is what the build-along does.

Real world

RouterBench and a licence check. Hu et al. (2024), "RouterBench: A Benchmark for Multi-LLM Routing System", collect "over 405k inference outcomes" across tasks such as MMLU, GSM8K, MBPP and MT-Bench. It is the bigger benchmark. Its code is MIT, but its dataset card on Hugging Face states no licence, and without a stated licence redistribution is not granted. So the paper is read and cited, and the rows shipped here are RouteLLM's Apache-2.0 ones. Check the licence of routing data before you copy it into a product.

Self-studyKiln's router is cheap and weak on purpose4 min▶

The router behind the widget is ours, not RouteLLM's. It is a logistic regression on 12 features of the prompt text alone: prompt length, line count, code marks, digit share, maths words, creative words, reasoning words, question marks, non-ASCII share, rewrite verbs, format words and word count. It was fit with scikit-learn on RouteLLM's train split to predict "the judge scored the small answer 4 or 5".

On the 2,000 sampled rows its AUC is 0.662 (computed). A router that only looks at prompt length scores 0.631, and the same scores scrambled across prompts score 0.529; 0.5 is a coin. So it carries a real but modest signal. That is deliberate: if a router this crude pays for itself, a trained one like RouteLLM's has room to do better, and you can see every moving part.

Part 3 · covers the frontier and the floor

Read the frontier, then draw the floor

5 min live

Plot every routing policy as one point: cost per 1,000 requests across, acceptable answers up. The quality bar is constructed: an answer is acceptable when the judge scored it 4 or 5, and the big model's answer is the judge's reference, so anything routed big counts as acceptable. Then draw a horizontal line at the quality you refuse to go below. For Kiln that floor is 95%. The question stops being "how much can we save" and becomes "what is the cheapest point above the line".

LiveTwo corners and a ceiling4 min▶

Everything big is Kiln today: $4.60 per 1,000 requests at 100.0% acceptable, by construction. Everything small is the anti-lever: $0.98 per 1,000, a 78.7% saving, and 87.1% acceptable, which ships 259 weak answers per 2,000 requests. It is the cheapest point on the chart and it is below the floor, so it is not an option, however good the saving looks on a slide.

Hindsight routes on the judge's score, which no router can know in advance. It sends 87.1% small, keeps 100.0% acceptable and costs $1.44, a 68.7% saving. It is a ceiling, not a target: the distance between it and your router is how much a better router could still earn.

LiveThe break button: does the router know anything?3 min▶

A router that saves money might simply be sending a fixed share of traffic small at random. To test that, scramble it: each prompt reads another prompt's score. The share sent small is unchanged, so the cost barely moves, but any real signal is destroyed. At threshold 0.87 the same 59.0% go small and acceptable falls from 95.7% to 92.7%, with 147 weak answers instead of 87 (computed).

The scrambled router can still clear the floor, but only by sending far less traffic small: its cheapest threshold above 95% is 0.91, which saves 26.7% against the learned router's 45.9%. That gap is what the signal is worth. Run this test on any router a vendor sells you.

Real world

The same router, a different benchmark, a different saving. RouteLLM's Table 6 reports cost-saving ratios at 50% PGR of 3.66x on MT Bench, 1.49x on GSM8K and 1.41x on MMLU. Same routers, same strong and weak model. Open-ended chat has many prompts the weak model handles fine; a knowledge exam has fewer. Your traffic mix decides where on that spread you land, which is why a published saving is a reason to measure, not a forecast.

Self-studyThe length router, a fair cheap rival3 min▶

Before trusting any learned router, compare it to the dumbest one that could work: short prompts go small. On these rows the cheapest length cut that clears the floor is "up to 50" estimated tokens, which gives 95.5% acceptable at $2.61 per 1,000, a 43.2% saving (computed). The learned router at 0.87 does a little better: 95.7% at $2.49 and 45.9%. A small margin. If your learned router cannot beat a length rule on your traffic, ship the length rule: it has nothing to retrain and nothing to drift.

Build-along

Route 2,000 real prompts at three thresholds

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Constructed Kiln example, real prompts. The widget scores each of the 2,000 RouteLLM rows with Kiln's cheap router, sends it small or big by your threshold, and prices the result with Kiln's constructed 1,200-token system prompt added to every request. The small model in these rows is Mixtral 8x7B, scored 1 to 5 by GPT-4 as judge; it is a different small model from the Llama 3.1 8B that sessions 3 and 4 serve, so the routing numbers teach the shape and your own small model needs its own measurement.

Start at threshold 0.85 and read the verdict line. Learned router, gpt-4.1 / gpt-4.1-mini prices, scramble off. It looks like a great deal until you read the quality tile.

★ Step 1 · threshold 0.85Sent to the small model: 67.4% (1,348 of 2,000) Acceptable answers: 93.4% (1,868 of 2,000) Weak answers shipped: 132 Cost per 1,000 requests: $2.15 saving 53.3% Verdict: fails Kiln's 95% floor.

Slide to 0.87. Fewer requests go small, quality climbs past the line, and the saving shrinks. This is the cheapest learned threshold that clears the floor.

★ Step 2 · threshold 0.87Sent to the small model: 59.0% (1,179 of 2,000) Acceptable answers: 95.7% (1,913 of 2,000) Weak answers shipped: 87 Cost per 1,000 requests: $2.49 saving 45.9% Verdict: clears. Cheapest threshold that does.

Slide to 0.90 to see what extra safety costs. Every step up buys quality with saving. Whether 97.4% is worth giving up the difference in saving is Theo's call, not the router's.

★ Step 3 · threshold 0.90Sent to the small model: 42.7% (854 of 2,000) Acceptable answers: 97.4% (1,947 of 2,000) Weak answers shipped: 53 Cost per 1,000 requests: $3.08 saving 33.0%

Go back to 0.87 and tick the break button. The share sent small stays put; the quality falls. That drop is the proof that the router's scores carry information about the prompt.

★ Step 4 · scrambled at 0.87Sent to the small model: 59.0% (unchanged) Acceptable answers: 92.7% (was 95.7%) Weak answers shipped: 147 (was 87) Router signal (AUC): 0.529 (learned: 0.662; a coin: 0.5)

Untick scramble and switch the price preset. Same routing, same quality, different price lists. The saving moves with the price gap between the two models.

★ Step 5 · the price gap decides the savinggpt-5 / gpt-5-mini, threshold 0.87: $2.21 per 1,000 vs $4.12 everything big saving 46.5% RouteLLM paper's 2024 prices (GPT-4 / Mixtral), threshold 0.87: $8.79 per 1,000 vs $20.49 everything big saving 57.1% everything small: $0.38 per 1,000, a 98.1% saving, still 87.1% acceptable

Write Kiln's routing line. One line Maya can paste into the plan, with the number that proves it and a date it stops being true.

★ Step 6 · Kiln's routing lineRoute with the learned router at threshold 0.87: 95.7% acceptable (floor 95%), 45.9% cheaper than everything on gpt-4.1. Quality judged by GPT-4 on Mixtral 8x7B answers; re-measure with our own small model. Re-measure monthly, and whenever a price, a model or the traffic mix changes.

Your numbers will differ. Your prompts, your small model, your judge and your price gap all move the frontier. Keep the method: a floor someone signed, the cheapest threshold above it, a scramble test, and a monthly re-measure.

Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. Label 100 of your own requests. Run each through your big and your candidate small model and score the small answer against the big one, by hand or with a judge model. That is your first frontier.
  2. Compute your price gap. Divide your big model's input and output prices by your small model's. Step 5 showed the saving tracks the gap.
  3. Pick your floor and who signs it. Write the acceptable-answer rate you will not go below, and the name of the person who owns that number. A floor nobody signed moves every time the bill hurts.
  4. Find your router's two error costs. What does one weak answer shipped cost you (a ticket, a churned user, a correction)? What does one wasted big call cost (the price difference)? The ratio tells you which side of the threshold to err on.
Check yourself

Three routing questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Sending everything to the small model saves 78.7%. Why is it not on Kiln's plan?

Everything small is the cheapest point on the chart and the anti-lever: $0.98 per 1,000 but 87.1% acceptable. The floor rules it out before cost is even compared.

2 · At threshold 0.87, scrambling the router keeps 59.0% going small but drops acceptable answers from 95.7% to 92.7%. What does that prove?

Scrambling keeps the share and the cost the same but destroys the link between prompt and score. Quality falls, so the link was doing work. AUC tells the same story: 0.662 learned against 0.529 scrambled.

3 · RouteLLM reports a 3.66x cost saving on MT Bench but 1.41x on MMLU, at the same 50% PGR. Why?

Same routers, same models, different traffic. Open-ended chat has more prompts the weak model handles; an exam has fewer. Your own traffic mix sets your saving.
