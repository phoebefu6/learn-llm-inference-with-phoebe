Batching and the GPU you rent

Session 1 showed that one decode step streams every weight out of memory to make a single token. The obvious move is to make that one read serve many requests at once. That is batching, and how you do it decides whether short answers sit idle behind long ones, how fast the queue grows as traffic rises, and what each request costs on a GPU you pay for by the hour whether it is busy or not. Tonight we find the highest load one rented L4 can carry for Kiln at a 1 second first-token target.

Part 1 · covers static and continuous batching

Wait for the bus, or let people hop on

5 min live

The first batching servers worked like a bus that only leaves when everyone on it has reached the last stop. A batch of requests starts together and runs, one decode step at a time, until its longest answer is finished. A request with a one-line answer finishes early and then its slot sits empty for the rest of the trip, and new arrivals wait outside until the whole batch is done. Continuous batching changes the unit of scheduling from the batch to the single decode step: after every step, finished requests leave and waiting ones take their place.

LiveWhy the step, not the batch, is the right unit3 min▶

Answers vary wildly in length. In Kiln's real rows a mean answer is 250 tokens, but some are a sentence and some are pages. Under static batching the cost of the batch is set by its longest member, so every short answer pays for the longest one in idle slots, and every new arrival pays for it in waiting time. Both show up in the place users feel: time to first token, because a request cannot start its prefill until it is admitted.

Continuous batching, also called iteration-level batching or in-flight batching, makes a scheduling decision after every decode step. A request that finished leaves; a request that is waiting joins, gets its prefill, and decodes alongside the others from the next step on. The GPU stays as full as memory allows, and a new request waits for one step rather than for the slowest stranger in the batch.

Real world

Orca named it, and the jump was large against its baseline. Yu et al. (2022), "Orca: A Distributed Serving System for Transformer-Based Generative Models" (OSDI 2022), proposed "iteration-level scheduling" plus "selective batching" (batching only the operations that can be batched across requests of different lengths). On GPT-3 175B they report a "36.9x throughput improvement at the same level of latency" against NVIDIA FasterTransformer, the serving system of that time. Read the multiplier as Orca against FasterTransformer in 2022, not as what continuous batching adds to a modern engine that already has it.

Self-studyWho does it today, and what they call it3 min▶

Continuous batching is now the default in every serious serving engine, which is why you rarely see it as a switch. vLLM schedules at the step level by design. Hugging Face's Text Generation Inference lists "Continuous batching of incoming requests" among its features, and its docs now say "text-generation-inference is now in maintenance mode", recommending vLLM and SGLang instead. NVIDIA's TensorRT-LLM calls the same idea "In-flight batching". Three names, one mechanism.

The practical lesson: if you rent a managed endpoint or run one of these engines, you already have continuous batching. Where static batching still bites is in hand-rolled serving code that loops over a fixed batch, and in offline jobs where nobody checked. The build-along lets you switch it back on to see what it costs.

Part 2 · covers throughput against the tail

More riders per trip, a longer wait at the stop

5 min live

Session 1 showed that one decode step reads all the weights once (what each job waits on). With a batch, that one read produces one token for every request in the batch, so tokens per second rise with batch size almost for free. The price is paid elsewhere. Every new arrival needs a prefill, and that prefill interrupts the decode steps of everyone already running. As load rises the batch grows, steps get slower, the queue grows, and the p95 time to first token climbs, slowly at first and then all at once.

LiveRead the five loads like Maya would3 min▶

All modelled, one L4, Kiln's request mix. At 0.25 requests a second the mean batch is 3.8 and the GPU puts out 70 tokens a second; the p95 first token is 656 ms. Triple the load to 0.75 and the mean batch grows to 17.0 and throughput to 206 tok/s, while p95 only moves to 957 ms. That is batching paying off: more work from the same weight reads, at a small cost in the tail.

Then the knee. At 1 request a second, p95 is 1.25 s and Kiln's 1 second target is missed, although the GPU is still producing more tokens (271 tok/s, batch 30.6). At 1.25 requests a second arrivals outpace what the GPU can finish, the queue never empties, and p95 is 53.3 s. Throughput is still rising (313 tok/s) at the very moment the service becomes unusable. That is why a throughput chart on its own is the wrong chart for capacity: the tail decides, and Learn Agent Launch treats that p95 as a service level you own (operational metrics ↗).

Real world

Sarathi-Serve attacked exactly this interruption. Agrawal et al. (2024), "Taming Throughput-Latency Tradeoff in LLM Inference with Sarathi-Serve", split long prefills into chunks ("chunked-prefills") and fit them between decode steps so running requests are not stalled ("stall-free scheduling"). Against vLLM they report 2.6x serving capacity for Mistral-7B on one A100, up to 3.7x for Yi-34B on two A100s and up to 5.6x for Falcon-180B. The idea won: vLLM's docs now say chunked prefill is "enabled by default whenever possible" in its V1 engine.

Self-studyChunked prefill: stop one long prompt freezing everyone3 min▶

In plain continuous batching a new request's whole prefill runs in one go, and every decode step behind it waits. Kiln's prompts carry a 1,200-token system prompt, so each arrival is a sizeable prefill. Chunked prefill cuts that prefill into pieces and runs one piece per step alongside the decode work, so time per output token for running users stays steady and the GPU's arithmetic, idle during decode, gets used.

The capacity model in the build-along does not chunk prefills. It treats each prefill as one block, which is one reason to read its tail numbers as a model of the mechanism, not a forecast for your engine. Session 5 removes most of the prefill another way, by reusing the system prompt's KV.

Self-studyDisaggregation: prefill and decode on different GPUs3 min▶

If prefill is arithmetic-bound and decode is bandwidth-bound, why make them share a GPU at all? Zhong et al. (2024), "DistServe" (OSDI 2024), run prefill on one set of GPUs and decode on another and hand the KV cache across. They report serving "7.4x more requests or 12.6x tighter SLO" against the systems they compare with. It is the right tool when you run many GPUs and have very different first-token and per-token targets; on one rented L4 there is nothing to split.

Self-studyEvery multiplier has its own baseline2 min▶

Orca 36.9x against FasterTransformer. Sarathi-Serve 2.6x to 5.6x against vLLM. DistServe 7.4x more requests against its comparison systems. PagedAttention, in session 4, 2-4x against FasterTransformer and Orca. Each paper picked its model, its hardware, its traffic and the best system of its time as the baseline. None of them multiplies with the others, and none of them tells you what your engine will gain, because your engine already includes most of the earlier ideas. The honest use of a multiplier is to know which mechanism matters, then measure on your own traffic.

Part 3 · covers utilisation and the flat line

The GPU bills by the hour, busy or not

5 min live

An API charges per token, so an idle hour costs nothing. A rented GPU is a flat line: $584 a month for one L4 at Hugging Face Endpoints' list price ($0.80 an hour for 730 hours, read 2026-11-08), whether it serves a million requests or none. Learn Fine-tuning and Small Language Models compared those two lines and assumed the L4 was fully usable (the break-even bench ↗). This Part asks the question that assumption skips: how much can that L4 actually serve at Kiln's 1 second target, and what does each request cost at that load?

LiveTurn a monthly price into a cost per request3 min▶

The rule is one division: the hourly price over the requests served in that hour. The hard part is the denominator, which is not "as many as the GPU can push" but "as many as it can push while the tail meets the target". On the L4 with FP8 weights the capacity model's sweep says 0.75 requests a second, which puts the cost at $0.30 per 1,000 requests ($1.08 per million output tokens). The same L4 with BF16 weights carries only 0.25 requests a second at the target, so the same $584 buys $0.89 per 1,000. Session 4 explains why the weight format triples the load.

Bigger cards look worse on the monthly line and better per request. The A100 serves 4 requests a second at the target for $0.17 per 1,000; the H100 serves 12 for $0.10. All modelled. The catch is in the last line of the figure: $0.10 is only true if Kiln actually sends the H100 12 requests a second, around the clock. Session 6 shows what happens to Kiln's bill when the small side's traffic is far below that.

Real world

Even the price of the flat line disagrees with itself. On 2026-11-08 Hugging Face's pricing page listed an H100 80GB endpoint on AWS at $4.50 an hour, while its Inference Endpoints docs page listed the H100 on GCP only, at $10 an hour. Both are Hugging Face's own pages. The capacity widget uses $4.50 and says when it was read. At $10 an hour (our arithmetic: more than twice $4.50) the H100's cost per request would more than double, and the comparison with the A100 would change. Before you sign, read the price on the page you will actually be billed from.

Self-studyStatic batching on the same L4: no load is fast enough3 min▶

Keep everything else fixed (L4, FP8 weights, Kiln's mix) and switch to static batching with each slot reserving 4,096 tokens of KV. The capacity model prints a p95 first-token time of 30.5 s at 0.25 requests a second and 48.4 s at 0.5. No load in the sweep meets 1 second. The GPU and its price are the same; what changed is how the work is scheduled, and the flat line now buys nothing usable.

Reserved KV on its own, with continuous batching kept on, is less dramatic but still costly: the L4 holds 22 concurrent requests instead of 57, p95 at 0.75 requests a second is 11.1 s, and the highest load meeting the target drops to 0.5, $0.44 per 1,000. That memory story is session 4's.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why does continuous batching help the tail of time to first token so much compared with static batching?

Under static batching a new arrival waits outside until the whole batch, including its longest answer, is finished. Continuous batching decides after every decode step, so the wait is roughly one step. In the build-along the same L4 goes from 30.5 s p95 (static, reserved) to 656 ms at 0.25 requests a second.

2 · Why does throughput rise as the batch grows on the same GPU?

Decode waits on memory bandwidth, not arithmetic. Sharing one weight read across a mean batch of 17.0 instead of 3.8 is close to free, which is why the modelled L4 goes from 70 tok/s at a mean batch of 3.8 to 206 tok/s at 17.0. Each request's own KV still has to be read, so it is not entirely free.

3 · The sweep prints $0.30 per 1,000 requests for an L4 with FP8 weights. When is that number wrong?

$0.30 is the hourly price divided by the requests served in that hour at 0.75 requests a second. At lower traffic the same month is spread over fewer requests, so each costs more; at higher traffic the 1 s target breaks. Only a full GPU earns its per-request price.
